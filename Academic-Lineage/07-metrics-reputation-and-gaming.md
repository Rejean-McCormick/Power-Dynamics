# Metrics, Reputation, Prestige, and Gaming

Any system that makes expertise, contribution, trust, or credibility legible creates a new power surface.

## Donald Campbell — indicators become corruption targets

Campbell's work on social indicators is associated with a recurring warning: when quantitative indicators become important targets for decision-making, actors have incentives to corrupt or optimize the indicator rather than the underlying goal.

**Correspondence:** EkoH, reputation, contribution metrics, moderation metrics, and SmartVote-derived standings must be treated as **adversarially optimizable surfaces**.

Reference: Donald T. Campbell, “Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change,” *Evaluation and Program Planning* 2(1), 1979. https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189(79)90048-X

## Robert K. Merton — the Matthew effect

Merton analyzed cumulative advantage in science: recognition and resources can disproportionately accrue to already prominent actors.

**Correspondence:** credibility systems can produce a positive feedback loop:

```text
initial recognition
→ more visibility
→ more opportunities
→ more visible contribution
→ more recognition
```

**Design implication:** distinguish demonstrated contribution from **recognition compounding**, and monitor whether discoverability causes prestige to become self-validating.

Reference: Robert K. Merton, “The Matthew Effect in Science,” *Science* 159(3810), 1968. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.159.3810.56

## Prestige, reputation, and authority are not identical

Power Dynamics should retain separate concepts for:

- observed performance;
- domain expertise;
- social prestige;
- contribution history;
- institutional credential;
- political standing;
- moral / ethical trust;
- brand affiliation.

A reputation system becomes dangerous when one of these silently converts into all the others.

## Proposed audit questions

For every score or ranking:

1. What exactly is being measured?
2. Is the construct domain-specific?
3. Can participants optimize the metric without improving the underlying objective?
4. Does early visibility create cumulative advantage?
5. Are negative evidence and uncertainty preserved?
6. Is the score portable, contestable, and versioned?
7. Can users inspect the weighting rule?
8. Does the score grant authority outside the domain it measures?
9. Can alternative scoring systems coexist?
10. What happens to newcomers with no historical data?

## Proposed invariant

> **Legibility should increase informed choice without turning measurement into universal sovereignty.**

The academic literature makes the gaming and cumulative-advantage risks difficult to dismiss. It does not determine the correct scoring architecture; that remains an engineering and governance question.
