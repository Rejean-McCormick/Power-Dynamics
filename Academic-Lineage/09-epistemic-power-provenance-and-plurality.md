# Epistemic Power, Provenance, Disagreement, and Plurality

Kristal and Konnaxion operate in a space that social epistemology, argumentation theory, and belief-revision research have studied from different angles.

## Miranda Fricker — epistemic injustice

Fricker distinguishes forms of injustice in which a speaker is given an unfair credibility deficit or lacks shared interpretive resources for making an experience intelligible.

**Correspondence:** credibility systems, semantic categories, moderation, and expert-weighted discovery can either reduce or amplify epistemic exclusion.

**Design implication:** a credibility system should inspect not only false positive authority, but also **systematic credibility deficits** and missing conceptual categories.

Reference: Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing*, Oxford University Press, 2007. https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198237907.001.0001

## Alvin Goldman — social epistemology and expert trust

Goldman's work treats knowledge practices as social systems that can be evaluated partly by how well they support truth-seeking and rational trust.

**Correspondence:** provenance, source comparison, expert-selection evidence, and visible disagreement should support users' own epistemic judgment rather than simply replace it with authority.

Reference: Alvin I. Goldman, “Experts: Which Ones Should You Trust?”, 2001. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00093.x

## AGM belief revision — changing a knowledge state should be explicit

Alchourrón, Gärdenfors, and Makinson formalized belief revision as a problem of changing a knowledge / belief set while preserving rational structure.

**Correspondence:** versioned Kristals, explicit revisions, lineage, conflict, supersession, and rollback are related to the general problem that **new information should alter a knowledge state without erasing how the change occurred**.

Reference: Carlos E. Alchourrón, Peter Gärdenfors, and David Makinson, “On the Logic of Theory Change: Partial Meet Contraction and Revision Functions,” *Journal of Symbolic Logic* 50(2), 1985. https://doi.org/10.2307/2274239

## Dung — argumentation frameworks and conflicting claims

Dung's abstract argumentation framework models arguments and attack relations without requiring all participants to share a single consistent knowledge base.

**Correspondence:** competing claims / Kristals can remain explicitly related through support, challenge, provenance, and evaluation rather than being collapsed into false consensus.

Reference: Phan Minh Dung, “On the Acceptability of Arguments and Its Fundamental Role in Nonmonotonic Reasoning, Logic Programming and n-Person Games,” *Artificial Intelligence* 77(2), 1995. https://doi.org/10.1016/0004-3702(94)00041-X

## Epistemic design implications

1. **Cryptographic integrity ≠ truth.**
2. **Provenance ≠ authority.**
3. **Source multiplicity ≠ source independence.**
4. **Forkability ≠ epistemic equivalence.**
5. **Consensus ≠ absence of dissent.**
6. **Credibility ≠ infallibility.**
7. **Revision should preserve lineage.**
8. **Competing worldviews should remain comparable where possible.**

The academic lineage reinforces the repo's adversarial epistemic tests. It also supplies a warning: making epistemic structure explicit does not eliminate disagreement; it makes disagreement more inspectable.
