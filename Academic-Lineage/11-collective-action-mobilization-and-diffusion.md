# Collective Action, Mobilization, Diffusion, and Thresholds

Power Dynamics includes a mobilization layer: resources do not become social power merely because they exist. They must be **released, routed, coordinated, and adopted**.

## Mancur Olson — collective action is not automatic

Olson's classic work shows why groups with shared interests may still fail to organize or contribute to collective goods because individual incentives and group outcomes can diverge.

**Correspondence:** kOA cannot assume that a good common objective automatically produces participation. Roles, incentives, visibility of contribution, modular tasks, local ownership, and low-friction entry all affect whether latent capacity becomes mobilized capacity.

Reference: Mancur Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action*, Harvard University Press, 1965. https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674041660

## McCarthy & Zald — resource mobilization

Resource Mobilization Theory shifts attention from grievances alone toward the resources, organizations, media relations, alliances, and structural conditions that make sustained collective action possible.

**Correspondence:** Power Dynamics' `Reservoir → Release → Flow → Mediation` model and the distinction between possessing resources and actually mobilizing them.

Reference: John D. McCarthy and Mayer N. Zald, “Resource Mobilization and Social Movements: A Partial Theory,” *American Journal of Sociology* 82(6), 1977. https://doi.org/10.1086/226464

## Granovetter — threshold models of collective behavior

Granovetter models individuals as having different participation thresholds: some act early, while others act only after enough others have acted first. Small differences in the distribution of thresholds can produce very different collective outcomes.

**Correspondence:** a network can contain substantial **latent commitment** that becomes visible only after credibility, adoption, or social proof crosses thresholds.

This directly supports analysis of:

- delayed activation of previously contacted collaborators;
- tipping dynamics;
- adoption cascades;
- why early visible participants can disproportionately affect later mobilization;
- why aggregate inactivity does not prove absence of latent support.

Reference: Mark Granovetter, “Threshold Models of Collective Behavior,” *American Journal of Sociology* 83(6), 1978. https://doi.org/10.1086/226707

## Everett Rogers — diffusion of innovations

Rogers' diffusion framework studies how innovations spread through communication channels over time and how uncertainty, social networks, and adopter categories affect uptake.

**Correspondence:** cultural / narrative corridors, demonstrations, reference implementations, peer testimony, and local adoption can be treated as parts of a diffusion system rather than generic “marketing.”

Reference: Everett M. Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations*, 5th ed., Free Press, 2003.

## Damon Centola — complex contagion

Centola's experimental work shows that behavioral adoption can differ from simple information diffusion: some behaviors benefit from **reinforcement through multiple social contacts** rather than one weak exposure.

**Correspondence:** mobilization for costly participation may require redundant trust paths and local reinforcement, even when information itself can spread through weak ties.

Reference: Damon Centola, “The Spread of Behavior in an Online Social Network Experiment,” *Science* 329(5996), 2010. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1185231

## Power Dynamics consequence

Mobilization should be modeled as a conversion problem:

```text
latent resource / willingness
→ visibility
→ credibility / social proof
→ threshold crossing
→ contribution
→ coordination
→ result
→ public evidence
→ lower thresholds for later participants
```

This creates a possible compounding loop, but also a capture risk: whoever controls visibility, endorsement, discovery, or narrative framing may control which latent resources ever become mobilized.
