# Polycentric Governance and Institutional Grammar

## Elinor Ostrom — polycentric governance

Ostrom's work rejects the assumption that complex collective-action problems must be solved either by a single state hierarchy or by simple privatization. Polycentric systems can contain multiple, partially autonomous centers of decision-making that interact, experiment, monitor, and learn.

**Correspondence:**

- federation and subsidiarity;
- local autonomy;
- independently governed branches;
- multiple implementations sharing protocols;
- `Founder-powered → Architecture-powered → Protocol-powered → Ecosystem-powered`.

**Design implication:** decentralization is not merely “no center.” A viable polycentric ecology still needs rules for coordination, conflict, monitoring, nested levels, and cross-scale learning.

References:
- Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*, Cambridge University Press, 1990.
- Elinor Ostrom, “Beyond Markets and States: Polycentric Governance of Complex Economic Systems,” Nobel Prize Lecture, 2009/2010. https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/lecture/

## Crawford & Ostrom — a grammar of institutions

Crawford and Ostrom proposed a structured grammar for analyzing institutional statements — rules, norms, and shared strategies.

**Correspondence:** kOA's ambition to represent institutional structures as explicit, inspectable, versionable artifacts rather than leaving them as tacit custom or inaccessible legal prose.

**Design implication:** common syntax can increase legibility without requiring identical institutions. This is a strong academic precedent for **shared grammar + local institutional variation**.

Reference: Sue E. S. Crawford and Elinor Ostrom, “A Grammar of Institutions,” *American Political Science Review* 89(3), 1995. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/grammar-of-institutions/7D37CD3BC5ED2D9FD57D2EE292958F47

## Institutional levels: operational, collective-choice, constitutional

The Institutional Analysis and Development tradition distinguishes rules that govern day-to-day action from rules governing collective choice and higher-level constitutional rules that determine who can alter the system itself.

**Correspondence:** Power Dynamics' authority architecture and the recurring question:

> **Who can change the rules that determine who can change the rules?**

**Design implication:** audits should classify authority by level, not merely by feature. A system can be democratic operationally while constitutional amendment remains concentrated.

## Polycentricity is not automatically non-dominating

A federation can still contain:

- a dominant reference implementation;
- a registry everyone depends on;
- an interface controlled by one maintainer group;
- a financially dominant node;
- a credibility system that becomes de facto mandatory.

Therefore:

> **Polycentric topology ≠ non-domination.**

Power Dynamics adds dependency, conversion, and exit analysis to the polycentric frame.
