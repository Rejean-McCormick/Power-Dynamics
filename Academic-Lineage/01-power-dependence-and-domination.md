# Power, Dependence, Domination, and Conversion

This lineage is the closest academic neighbor to the repo's distinction between **capability** and **relational power**.

## Robert A. Dahl — relational power

Dahl's 1957 formulation treats power as a relation in which A can affect B's behavior. This is a useful baseline for Power Dynamics because it rejects `power = possession` and directs attention toward **relations, scope, and effects**.

**Correspondence:** `Resource → Potential → Capacity → Puissance → Power Relation → Effect`.

**Limit:** Dahl's behavioral formulation is narrower than Power Dynamics' interest in agenda formation, infrastructures, dependency, and latent possibility.

Reference: Robert A. Dahl, “The Concept of Power,” *Behavioral Science* 2(3), 1957. https://doi.org/10.1002/bs.3830020303

## Bachrach & Baratz; Steven Lukes — visible decisions are not the whole power surface

Bachrach and Baratz's “two faces” argument expands power analysis beyond observable decisions to **nondecision and agenda control**. Lukes' later three-dimensional account adds preference-shaping and forms of domination that may not present as open conflict.

**Correspondence:** the **pre-political chain** in Power Dynamics: classification, discoverability, ranking, agenda formation, defaults, and legitimacy can shape outcomes before a formal vote occurs.

**Design implication:** an audit that only inspects formal decision rights will miss important upstream power.

References:
- Peter Bachrach and Morton S. Baratz, “Two Faces of Power,” *American Political Science Review* 56(4), 1962. https://doi.org/10.2307/1952796
- Steven Lukes, *Power: A Radical View*, first published 1974; later editions expand the argument. https://www.bloomsbury.com/ca/power-9781350544758/

## Richard M. Emerson — power-dependence relations

Emerson's 1962 theory is exceptionally close to the repo's dependency logic: power emerges from a relation in which one actor depends on resources, services, or opportunities controlled by another, and dependence is reduced when meaningful alternatives exist.

**Correspondence:**

- `Strong capability. Weak sovereignty.`
- `credible exit`;
- substitutability;
- alternative providers / branches;
- dependency-exit audits;
- founder decentering through reproducibility.

**Design implication:** for each critical capability, ask not only *who can provide it?* but also *who becomes dependent on that provider, and what alternatives remain viable?*

Reference: Richard M. Emerson, “Power-Dependence Relations,” *American Sociological Review* 27(1), 1962, pp. 31–41. https://www.jstor.org/stable/2089716

## Cook, Emerson, Gillmore & Yamagishi — network structure changes power

Cook et al. experimentally studied power in exchange networks and found that simple centrality measures can fail where dependency structure predicts power more accurately.

**Correspondence:** Power Dynamics should not infer `most connected = most powerful`. Network power depends on **alternatives, scarcity, exchange structure, and dependency topology**.

**Design implication:** network audits should combine centrality with dependency / substitutability measures.

Reference: Karen S. Cook, Richard M. Emerson, Mary R. Gillmore, and Toshio Yamagishi, “The Distribution of Power in Exchange Networks,” *American Journal of Sociology* 89(2), 1983. https://doi.org/10.1086/227866

## Pfeffer & Salancik — resource dependence

Resource Dependence Theory studies how organizations become constrained by actors controlling critical external resources.

**Correspondence:** DNS, hosting, registries, funding, compute, signing keys, maintainer knowledge, payment rails, distribution, trademarks, and scarce operators can all create hidden sovereignty even when governance documents appear decentralized.

**Design implication:** extend the power audit from formal authority to **critical-resource dependency**.

Reference: Jeffrey Pfeffer and Gerald R. Salancik, *The External Control of Organizations: A Resource Dependence Perspective*, 1978. https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/books/external-control-organizations-resource-dependence-perspective

## French & Raven — distinct bases of social power

French and Raven distinguished bases of social influence such as legitimate, reward, coercive, expert, and referent power.

**Correspondence:** Power Dynamics' separation of domains and conversion firewalls. Expertise, money, brand, technical administration, and political standing should not be treated as interchangeable permissions.

**Design implication:** identify the **source** of leverage before deciding the legitimate **scope** of that leverage.

Reference: John R. P. French Jr. and Bertram Raven, “The Bases of Social Power,” in *Studies in Social Power*, 1959. https://cdn.isr.umich.edu/pubFiles/historicPublications/studiesinsocialpower_1413_.pdf

## Pierre Bourdieu — multiple forms of capital and convertibility

Bourdieu distinguishes economic, cultural, and social forms of capital and emphasizes that resources can be accumulated and converted across social fields. His broader work on symbolic power is especially relevant to cases where recognition and classification become consequential without direct coercion.

**Correspondence:** Power Dynamics' cross-domain conversion model: money, network position, knowledge, credentials, reputation, and symbolic standing can become leverage in other domains.

**Design implication:** conversion pathways need explicit firewalls because different resources do not remain in the domains where they were originally earned.

Reference: Pierre Bourdieu, “The Forms of Capital,” in John G. Richardson (ed.), *Handbook of Theory and Research for the Sociology of Education*, Greenwood, 1986, pp. 241–258.

## Philip Pettit — non-domination

Republican non-domination focuses not merely on actual interference, but on exposure to **arbitrary power**: another actor may possess uncontrolled capacity to interfere even if it is not presently exercised.

**Correspondence:** `non-domination-terminality`, revocability, contestability, exit, and constitutional limits on successful functions becoming terminal sovereigns.

**Design implication:** “the administrator is benevolent” is not a sufficient safeguard. The architecture should reduce arbitrary dependency itself.

Reference: Philip Pettit, *Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government*. Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/0198296428.001.0001

## Peter Morriss — power as ability

Morriss' philosophical analysis is especially relevant to the repo's use of **puissance**. He argues that power is usefully understood as a form of **ability / capacity**, not only as successful influence over another actor.

**Correspondence:** this is a close conceptual neighbor to the repo's insistence that `capacity / puissance` must be distinguished from an exercised `power relation`.

**Limit:** similar vocabulary does not imply identity between Morriss' analysis and the Power Dynamics ontology.

Reference: Peter Morriss, *Power: A Philosophical Analysis*, Manchester University Press, 1987.

## Michel Foucault — power relations revealed through resistance

Foucault's “The Subject and Power” emphasizes studying concrete relations of power and using forms of resistance to locate their points of application.

**Correspondence:** counterpower, contestation, founder-opposition rehearsals, and adversarial audits can be used diagnostically: resistance is not merely a threat to the system; it is evidence about where power actually sits.

Reference: Michel Foucault, “The Subject and Power,” *Critical Inquiry* 8(4), 1982. https://doi.org/10.1086/448181

## Crozier & Friedberg — zones of uncertainty

Crozier and Friedberg's strategic analysis of organizations emphasizes how actors gain leverage through organizational **zones of uncertainty** — areas where rules do not fully determine outcomes and where some actors control information, expertise, external relations, or operational contingencies.

**Correspondence:** hidden maintainer knowledge, undocumented exceptions, manual override paths, scarce operational expertise, and discretionary queues can create power even when formal permissions are symmetrical.

**Design implication:** audit uncertainty control, not only explicit roles.

Reference: Michel Crozier and Erhard Friedberg, *L'acteur et le système: les contraintes de l'action collective*, Seuil, 1977.

## Power conversion as the synthesis opportunity

The literature above generally studies **particular relations or domains**. Power Dynamics adds a cross-domain question:

> **How does legitimate capability in one domain convert into leverage in another?**

Examples include:

```text
expertise → credibility → agenda leverage
brand → attention → affiliation → revenue
money → infrastructure → dependency
technical administration → defaults → governance leverage
network brokerage → opportunity allocation
```

The academic lineage strengthens the need for **Power Conversion Firewalls**. It does not by itself establish that the current firewalls are sufficient.
